\section{Introduction to Fourier transform}
In mathematics, the Fourier transform (FT) is an integral transform that acts on a function to reveal its frequency components. It takes an input function and generates a new, complex-valued function that details the intensity of different frequencies present in the original. The term ``Fourier transform'' applies to both the mathematical process itself and the resulting function. To distinguish between the two, the output is often referred to as the ``frequency domain'' representation. 

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{Images/fourier_transform.png}
    \caption{We can see how a signal in the Time domain consists of multiple signal of different frequencies}
    \label{fig:placeholder}
\end{figure}

 This process is similar to breaking a musical chord down to identify the volume of the individual notes that create it. The Fourier transform, $\hat{f}(\omega)$, of a function $f(t)$ is defined mathematically as:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{f}(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t) e^{-i\omega t} \, dt
\end{equation}

\section{Fast Furrier transform in general}
The Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) is an efficient algorithm used to compute the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) of a sequence, which transforms a signal from the time domain into the frequency domain. This means it breaks down a complex signal into its constituent sinusoidal components-frequencies and their amplitudes-making it easier to analyze patterns such as pitch in audio, frequencies in communications, or periodicities in data. The FFT drastically reduces the computational complexity of DFT from $O(N^2)$ to $O(N\log(N))$ by cleverly reusing calculations through a divide-and-conquer approach. This algorithm is ideal for real-life applications like audio processing, image analysis, wireless communication and other biomedical and industrial applications.


The most common FFT algorithm is the \textbf{Cooley-Tukey} algorithm, which is widely used due to its simplicity and efficiency. It works best when the input size $N$ is a power of 2. The most common versions of the algorithm are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Radix-2}: The most basic and common version. Splits the DFT into two halves (even and odd indices) at each stage. Requires $N$ to be a power of 2.
    \item \textbf{Radix-4}: More efficient than radix-2 when $N$ is a power of 4. Reduces the number of multiplications compared to radix-2 by grouping four elements at a time.
    \item \textbf{Split radix}: This combines both radix-2 and radix-4 versions in order to improuve efficiency.
\end{itemize}

\section{Radix-2 FFT Decomposition}

For a sequence $x[n]$, the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] \cdot W_N^{kn}, \quad \text{where } W_N = e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{N}}
\end{equation}
The term $W_N$ is referred to as the \textbf{twiddle factor}. The FFT algorithm reorganizes this summation to significantly reduce computational complexity.

By splitting the input $x[n]$ into even-indexed ($e[n] = x[2n]$) and odd-indexed ($o[n] = x[2n+1]$) samples, the DFT can be rewritten as:
\begin{equation}
    X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N/2 - 1} x[2n] \cdot W_N^{k \cdot 2n} + \sum_{n=0}^{N/2 - 1} x[2n+1] \cdot W_N^{k \cdot (2n+1)}
\end{equation}
Utilizing the property $W_N^{2kn} = W_{N/2}^{kn}$, this allows for the decomposition:
\begin{equation}
    X[k] = \underbrace{\sum_{n=0}^{N/2 - 1} e[n] \cdot W_{N/2}^{kn}}_{\text{Even terms } (E[k])} + W_N^k \cdot \underbrace{\sum_{n=0}^{N/2 - 1} o[n] \cdot W_{N/2}^{kn}}_{\text{Odd terms } (O[k])}
\end{equation}
Let $E[k]$ denote the DFT of the even-indexed inputs and $O[k]$ the DFT of the odd-indexed inputs. The equation simplifies to:
\begin{equation}
    X[k] = E[k] + W_N^k \cdot O[k]
\end{equation}
Due to the periodicity of the DFT (period $N$), the calculation for the full range $k = 0, 1, \dots, N-1$ can be computed using the \textbf{butterfly operation}:
\begin{align}
    X[k] &= E[k] + W_N^k \cdot O[k] \\
    X\left[k + \frac{N}{2}\right] &= E[k] - W_N^k \cdot O[k]
\end{align}
for $k = 0, 1, \dots, N/2 - 1$.

For an input sequence of length $N$, where $N = 2^m$, the FFT requires $m = \log_2(N)$ stages. Each stage performs these butterfly operations to progressively combine and transform the input data.

\section{Radix-4 FFT Decomposition}

The Radix-4 FFT extends the Radix-2 algorithm by dividing the DFT into \textbf{four smaller DFTs}. This approach requires the input size $N$ to be a power of 4 ($N=4^m$).

We group $x[n]$ into four interleaved subsequences:
\begin{align}
    x_0[n] &= x[4n], & x_1[n] &= x[4n+1], \nonumber \\
    x_2[n] &= x[4n+2], & x_3[n] &= x[4n+3]
\end{align}
The DFT expansion then becomes:
\begin{equation}
    X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N/4-1} \left[ 
    x[4n] + x[4n+1] W_N^k + x[4n+2] W_N^{2k} + x[4n+3] W_N^{3k}
    \right] W_{N/4}^{kn}
\end{equation}
This can be expressed as a linear combination of four quarter-length DFTs ($X_0, X_1, X_2, X_3$):
\begin{equation}
    X[k] = X_0[k] + W_N^k X_1[k] + W_N^{2k} X_2[k] + W_N^{3k} X_3[k]
\end{equation}
To simplify the 4-point butterfly operation, we define the intermediate terms:
\[
    A = X_0[k], \quad B = W_N^k X_1[k], \quad C = W_N^{2k} X_2[k], \quad D = W_N^{3k} X_3[k]
\]

The butterfly outputs are then calculated as:
\begin{align}
    X[k] &= A + B + C + D \\
    X\left[k + \frac{N}{4}\right] &= A - jB - C + jD \\
    X\left[k + \frac{N}{2}\right] &= A - B + C - D \\
    X\left[k + \frac{3N}{4}\right] &= A + jB - C - jD
\end{align}
Radix-4 exploits additional symmetries to reduce the total operation count. It reduces the number of stages to $\log_4 N$ (compared to $\log_2 N$ for Radix-2), providing greater efficiency in hardware implementations when $N$ is a power of 4.

\section{Unrolled vs SDF hardware design}
The are different approaches in designing the FFT algorythm in hardware, the most intuitive way is the unrolled way, this exploits its inherent parallelism
by unrolling the entire computation. Each butterfly operation is assigned a dedicated hardware unit, resulting in a fully unrolled design where all butterfly computations are executed concurrently. This method is very demanding in resourches especially for smaller fpgas, also it requires a very high bandwidth of data as it computes, for a 256 point fft, 256 data points at the same clock cycle.

In this chapter we will see how the original radix-2 SDF version of the FFT unit worked and the steps taken to impouve uppon this design in order to achive more bandwidth, smaller latency and faster frequencies.

\section{Initial version: Radix-2 Single path Delay Feedback}
In the Microprocessor Design course, we implemented a Single-Path Delay Feedback (SDF) FFT hardware unit based on the Radix-2 algorithm. This design represented a significant improvement over fully unrolled architectures, which introduced high design complexity due to the need for memory controllers to buffer input samples. Furthermore, unrolled designs incurred prohibitive resource utilization for FFTs with window sizes exceeding 256 points.

Although the initial Radix-2 SDF architecture resolved the scalability and resource issues, it introduced specific bottlenecks regarding performance and efficiency. First, the architecture processes one sample per clock cycle, requiring the samples to be stored in a feedback loop until threir pair arrives for computation. Second, while the butterfly unit generates two outputs simultaneously, the single-path nature of the design allows only one to pass to the next stage immediately, forcing the second result to be buffered. Third, the butterfly unit itself was implemented as a purely combinational circuit without internal pipelining, this significantly increased the critical path delay and limited the maximum operating frequency. 
Fourth, the data size of the samples and the twiddles was 32 and 16 bits and testing with smaller data sizes had not been done. Finally, the radix-2 architecture is not the fastest nor the most resourfull and the use of something better seemed inevitable.

\subsection{Twiddle factors}
Since our hardware implementations are designed for FFTs of fixed sizes, there is
no need for the algorithm to compute the twiddle factors at runtime. Calculating these
factors involves complex arithmetic operations, which are computationally expensive and
consume valuable FPGA resources. To optimize efficiency, all twiddle factors are pre-
computed during compilation and stored in on-chip memory. Because the FFT algorithm
operates using multiple computational units in parallel, the choice of memory architecture
is critical. The memory must provide sufficient bandwidth to avoid becoming a bottleneck
in the data path. To meet these performance requirements, twiddle factors are stored us-
ing distributed memory such as LUTRAM or flip-flops, enabling constant-time access and
ensuring that each computational unit can retrieve the required constants without delay.
This approach to storing twiddle factors will not change in the next designs.

\subsection{Datatypes}
In the initial design, the samples are represented using a 32-bit fixed-point format, where the value 1.0
corresponds to the integer 32767 (i.e., $2^{15} - 1$). Although the data is 32 bits wide, the
effective scaling is based on the Q1.15 format. This approach combines the benefits of
wider data paths such as reduced overflow risk and better intermediate precision with
the simplicity and hardware efficiency of fixed-point arithmetic.

\section{New version: Radix-4 Single path Delay Feedback}
To improve the latency of the design defined here as the total number of clock cycles required to process an entire fft window i selected the Radix-4 architecture. Theoretically, this architecture is faster because it requires fewer computational stages ($\log_4 N$) compared to Radix-2 ($\log_2 N$).

However, simply substituting the architecture within the old design was insufficient to yield performance gains. Processing one sample per clock with Radix-4 actually increased latency, as each butterfly unit incurred significant idle time waiting to acquire the four necessary data points to begin computation. Figures 1.5 and 1.6 illustrate the specific sample index at which computation begins for the second stage of each unit. In the Radix-2 architecture, computation starts as soon as the second sub-butterfly ,corresponding to the next stage, outputs its values. In contrast, for the Radix-4 architecture, this process is delayed until the fourth sub-butterfly has output its values. This results in a considerably longer wait time (latency) before the subsequent stage can begin processing.

To mitigate this, I increased the throughput to process four samples per clock cycle. This aligns perfectly with the Radix-4 butterfly's requirement for four parallel inputs, allowing computations to commence immediately. Implementing this parallelization required significant restructuring of the memory buffers to coordinate data alignment, as well as a complete redesign of the control logic to synchronize the system.

\subsection{Butterfly unit}
The Radix-4 butterfly unit is significantly more complex and resource-intensive than the Radix-2 unit, as it processes four samples simultaneously to better exploit the symmetries of the FFT algorithm. However, this increased complexity extends the combinatorial path for each input, leading to a substantially longer critical path. To mitigate this, I pipelined the design, breaking the computation into smaller stages so that only a specific set of parallel operations is performed in each clock cycle, thereby preserving the operating frequency.

\subsection{Control Unit}
The control unit is responsible for coordinating where inputs are stored, when butterfly operations start, and how the delay buffers function. This is achieved using two counters of variable size, which depends on the stage number.

The first is the stride segment counter, which times the writing of new inputs to memory and tracks when the second counter should start. This counter increments every time four new samples arrive (shown as green lines in the graph).

The second is the butterfly operation counter, which starts when the samples required for the butterfly operation arrive. These are the blue samples in the graph they represent the last samples of a butterfly cluster needed to complete the set of four inputs. Since only one physical butterfly unit exists, we cannot process all four operations at once even if we have the data. Therefore, only one operation happens at a time, and the other data must wait.

To make the design simpler and more portable, these counters do not iterate through all operations in a stage. Instead, each counter counts up to the number of butterflies in the respective cluster (in the graph, this number is 4). The length of these counters is calculated dynamically based on parameters from the SDF unit module that indicate the current stage.

Finally, there are other signals, such as the butterfly counter enable, which validates that the inputs and outputs of the butterfly are valid values and not junk. There is also a flush counter that tracks the number of remaining operations when the input enable signal goes low. This is needed because the unit requires extra time to finish processing after the last input is received due to the initial delay while waiting for values to arrive.

\subsection{Delay buffers/Memory}
The delay buffers are the most critical component of the design, as they facilitate the storing, rearranging, and timing of the data. They are essentially shift registers that delay the data by the correct number of cycles. Each buffer has four input ports and one output port. The inputs are always written to specific positions in the delay buffer ($0$, $\text{buffer\_size}/4$, $2 \cdot \text{buffer\_size}/4$, and $3 \cdot \text{buffer\_size}/4$); this is necessary to queue the data correctly.

A \texttt{write\_enable} signal is raised based on the \textit{stride segment counter}, instructing the buffer to store the inputs. Similarly, \texttt{read\_enable\_1} and \texttt{read\_enable\_2} signals are raised based on the \textit{butterfly enable counter} to retrieve data. In essence, these signals dictate the operating mode of the shift registers for any given clock cycle.

Distinct modes are required to time the data correctly. Since the patterns of incoming and outgoing data differ, a simple shifting buffer would lead to data overwrites, as the unit sometimes needs to write to locations where valid data has not yet been computed. To solve this, I implemented a \textbf{secondary shift register} in each module. This auxiliary register temporarily stores incoming data that would otherwise overwrite useful entries. When the timing is right, the design rotates or combines the two shift registers according to the \textit{stride segment counter}.

In each stage, four different delay buffers with slightly different timing are used to serve the data exactly as required. These buffers were custom-designed to match the algorithm's timing perfectly, ensuring that not a single extra register was used. While this approach worked well in theory, we will see later that the complexity of these custom shift registers caused issues with the synthesis tool. Given that this module is scheduled for future revision, a detailed operational breakdown is omitted here. The complexity of the data flow is difficult to illustrate sufficiently within a static report. A more comprehensive explanation will be demonstrated during the presentation.

\section{DSP utilization}
The butterfly unit performs intensive multiplication operations that can take advantage of the silicon DSP IP blocks embedded in most modern FPGAs. DSPs (Digital Signal Processing slices) are dedicated hardware modules designed to execute fast multiplication and Multiply-Accumulate (MAC) operations without consuming the general-purpose FPGA fabric. These hardened modules perform significantly faster than typical LUT-based multipliers. Furthermore, using them optimizes the design by reducing power consumption and freeing up programmable logic resources for control and storage tasks. Althought previous designs used DSPs, while pushing the designs to each limits a critical paths violation including the DSPs routing was accuring with cobmination of this error

\section{Multipliers in LUTs}
After adding extra pipeline stages to the design, an unexpected issue emerged: the DSP routing was unable to meet the required timing constraints. Although the butterfly schematic shows no chained multiplications suggesting that two DSPs should not be connected in series.

Specifically, the synthesis tool utilized 6 of the 12 butterflies strictly as multipliers, while the remaining 6 were configured as Multiply-Accumulate (MAC) units. This configuration forces the latter units to wait for the results of the former before performing the accumulate operation, creating a serial dependency that violates the intended parallel design and causes the timing failure.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{Images/BUtterfly_critical_path.png}
    \caption{Chained DSPs used for multiply and multiply accumulate}    
    \label{fig:placeholder}
\end{figure}

\newpage{}

To solve this i implimented  a Carry-Save Adder (CSA) architecture to perform signed multiplication without using dedicated DSP slices. It breaks the multiplication into a series of pipeline stages, where each stage corresponds to a bit of the multiplier b. Instead of performing a slow, full addition at every step, each stage calculates a Sum and a Carry vector simultaneously using bitwise XOR and majority logic. These vectors are passed through a pipeline (sum\_pipe and carry\_pipe) along with the original inputs to reduce the combinational path delay. In the final stage, the module accounts for signed arithmetic by subtracting the last partial product if the multiplier's sign bit is set, before performing a final addition to merge the sum and carry vectors into the completed product.

\section{New memory structure}
Finally, the initial design of the memory and delay buffers was inefficient, as it shifted all data from register to register on every clock cycle. Although shift registers are typically simple and fast, the complexity of these specific buffers likely hindered the synthesis tool's optimization. To resolve this, I implemented a more traditional memory architecture where the data remains stationary, and read/write pointers manage the data organization. These pointers are controlled by the two counters discussed previously.

Since this simpler memory design was more robust, I aimed to make it compatible with Block RAM (BRAM) modules. To achieve BRAM inference, I had to ensure that each memory block utilized no more than one write port and one read port (Simple Dual-Port) per physical BRAM resource. Given that the design requires four reads and four writes per cycle, I had to carefully structure the memory banks to ensure that no combination of simultaneous accesses exceeded the BRAM hardware capabilities. Consequently, instead of storing incoming samples in a linear sequence (e.g., $[0 \to 3]$, $[4 \to 7]$), I implemented a custom addressing scheme:

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{Images/memory_assecess.png}
    \caption{Memory module organization}
    \label{fig:placeholder}
\end{figure}

The following describes the memory architecture and its operation. The numbers represent the arrival order of the samples, which are fed into the unit in batches of four (e.g., $[1 \to 4], [5 \to 8]$, etc.). The memory is partitioned into four banks to enable simultaneous parallel reads and writes.

The green lines illustrate the write patterns; writing begins at the green pointer's head and concludes at the pointer's tail. The remaining lines represent the read patterns, which are designed to span across all four banks. By rotating or shifting the starting point of the write operation for every other line, I avoided a vertical alignment of the read patterns. Without this rotation, the system would attempt to read four values from a single bank simultaneously, which is impossible with standard dual-port BRAM. While increasing the number of banks could solve this, I chose this shifting approach to keep resource and register utilization as low as possible.

\section{Verification Methodology}

To verify the functional correctness of the FFT processor, the hardware outputs were compared against a golden model generated using the \texttt{numpy.fft} library. The verification environment was implemented as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stimulus Generation:} Random and synthetic test signals were generated in Python to ensure comprehensive coverage of the design space.
    \item \textbf{Data Quantization:} These signals were preprocessed and quantized to match the specific fixed-point datatypes and word lengths utilized in the hardware implementation.
    \item \textbf{Testbench Automation:} A HDL testbench was developed to read the input vectors, drive the simulation, and log the resulting hardware outputs into a \texttt{.txt} file for post-processing.
\end{itemize}
